How to price a handyman job without losing money

Small jobs are where solo handymen most often lose money: the drive, the hardware-store run and the "quick fix" that takes three hours all go unpaid. A quote built from the same few parts every time fixes most of that.

The five parts of a quote

  1. Labor: your estimated hours times your hourly rate. Estimate honestly, then add a buffer for jobs where the problem is hidden (e.g. behind a wall).
  2. Materials: what you actually pay, plus a markup (many tradespeople use 10-30%) to cover the time spent buying, carrying and returning items. Pick a markup and tell the customer your quotes include it.
  3. Trip fee: a flat amount, or miles driven times a per-mile cost, so travel is never free.
  4. Minimum charge: a floor (for example two hours of labor) so that a 20-minute job still covers the drive and the paperwork.
  5. Deposit: for jobs with materials, ask for part up front so you are not financing the customer's project.

A worked example

Replacing a bathroom faucet. Assumed numbers: 2 hours at $60/hour, a $85 faucet with a 20% markup, a $15 trip fee, and a $120 minimum.

  • Labor: 2 × $60 = $120
  • Materials: $85 × 1.20 = $102
  • Trip fee: $15
  • Total: $237 (above the $120 minimum, so the minimum doesn't apply)
  • Deposit at 50% of materials: $51

Your own numbers will differ by region and skill. The point is the structure.

Common mistakes

  • Quoting from the hourly rate you wish you had rather than one that covers insurance, tools, vehicle costs and slow weeks.
  • Forgetting unpaid time: quoting, driving, shopping and invoicing are real work.
  • No written quote. A short written quote avoids arguments about what was agreed.
  • Not setting money aside for tax. Self-employed income is usually taxed after you've been paid, so check with a tax professional about what percentage to reserve.
