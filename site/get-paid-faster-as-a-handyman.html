How to get paid faster as a handyman

Finishing the work is only half the job. A quote that is accepted but never paid costs you time, and chasing money is time you can't bill. Most late payment comes from vague terms and slow invoicing, both of which you control.

1. Agree the terms before you start

Put these on the quote, in plain words, and get a written yes (a text or email reply counts as a record):

  • What is included and what would count as extra work.
  • The price, or the rate and an estimated range.
  • When payment is due, for example "on completion" or "within 7 days".
  • How you accept payment: bank transfer, card, cash.

2. Consider a deposit for materials

If a job needs materials you must buy up front, asking the customer to cover them (or a percentage of the quote) before you start is common practice. It keeps you from financing someone else's project, and it filters out people who aren't serious. Keep it proportionate and write down exactly what it covers. Some places limit deposit sizes for certain work, so check your local rules.

3. Invoice the same day

Send the invoice before you leave the driveway or that evening. The longer you wait, the longer the customer waits to think about paying. Include the job description, the quote reference, the amount, the due date and how to pay. Make it easy: the fewer steps between "got the invoice" and "paid", the faster it happens.

4. Use a simple follow-up routine

  1. Due date: a friendly note, "Just a reminder the invoice is due today, thanks again."
  2. 3 to 5 days late: a short second message with the invoice attached again.
  3. 10 to 14 days late: a phone call. Stay polite and ask when you can expect payment.
  4. After that: a written notice of the next step, and look up your local small-claims process. Don't threaten anything you won't do.

Write the date of every reminder down so you have a record if it goes further.

5. Track what is outstanding

You can't chase what you can't see. Keep one list of every job with its status: quoted, accepted, done, invoiced, paid. Review it weekly and note the days since each invoice went out. The jobs stuck at "invoiced" are your follow-up list.

Common mistakes

  • Starting on a verbal "sounds good" with no written price.
  • Letting small extras slide without telling the customer they change the price.
  • Invoicing at the end of the month instead of per job.
  • Feeling awkward about reminders. A polite reminder is normal business.
